House mouse biology and winter behavior in Lubbock, TX, why they always come back.

Written by Lubbock Rodent Control. Updated November 2026.
By Lubbock Rodent Control · Published November 17, 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • One mouse becomes many fast. Texas A&M says a single mouse can have about 4,500 descendants in a year, so act on the first sign.
  • A mouse fits through a hole about the size of a dime, or a quarter inch crack under a door.
  • A house mouse usually stays within 20 to 50 feet of its nest, so droppings tell you where the nest is close by.
  • Traps work best along walls, after a period of pre-baiting, and in numbers: about six traps spaced at least three feet apart.
  • Sealing entries is what keeps mice from coming back each fall.

Why house mice are so hard to get rid of

House mice are built to live beside people. They eat almost anything, nest in anything soft, and need very little space. A pantry, a cabinet base or the gap behind a dishwasher is enough.

The bigger issue is how quickly they breed. Texas A&M puts the offspring from one mouse at about 4,500 in a year. You do not need to remember the figure. What matters is that a small problem in October can be a large one by spring if nothing is done.

That is why we tell people to treat the first dropping as the start of a problem, not a one off.

Why mice move indoors when it gets cold

Mice look for food, shelter and warmth. When outdoor food gets scarcer and nights get colder, buildings become the best option. Garages, crawl spaces, attics and kitchens are the common targets.

This is general mouse behavior, not something unique to one city. In Lubbock, as anywhere, the practical lesson is to check the building before the first hard cold, not after you hear noise in the wall.

How far a mouse travels

Texas A&M puts the range of a house mouse at about 20 to 50 feet. That is small. It means the nest is usually close to where you find droppings, often in the same room or the wall beside it.

Use that to narrow the search. Start with the nearest wall, cabinet base, appliance back or stack of stored boxes.

What size gap lets a mouse in

A dime sized hole is enough, and a quarter inch crack under a door will do, according to Texas A&M. Common entries are around pipes and gas lines, at dryer vents, beside garage door corners and where cables enter the house.

Walk the outside of the building at ground level and look for any gap you could slide a pencil into. Then check inside, under sinks and behind appliances.

Trapping that works

Mice are cautious about new objects. Texas A&M advises leaving traps unset with bait for one to two weeks first, so mice get used to them. Then set them along walls, where mice run with their whiskers touching the surface.

For a mouse problem the guide calls for about six traps, spaced at least three feet apart. Check them every day. A trap left for a week can hold a dead mouse that attracts flies and other pests.

Cleaning up safely

Droppings and nests can carry disease. The CDC says to ventilate a closed space for 30 minutes, wear gloves, spray droppings with a solution of 1.5 cups of bleach per gallon of water, let it sit for five minutes and then wipe up. Do not sweep or vacuum.

Bag the waste and wash your hands. For larger areas, attics or soaked insulation, call a crew.

When to call a professional

If you have trapped for two weeks with no drop in activity, there is probably an entry you have not found. If you hear animals in the walls or see droppings in several rooms, there is likely a population, not a straggler.

A locally owned crew can inspect for free, find the entries and seal them. We offer same-day service when you call early, and our line is open 24/7.

A week by week plan for a mouse problem

Week one is for finding. Walk the house with a flashlight, note every sign, and mark the places where you see droppings. Seal nothing yet, and leave traps unset with a little bait so mice get used to them, as Texas A&M advises.

Week two continues the pre-baiting while you fix what you can: put food away, move pet bowls in at night and repair leaks. Week three is for setting the traps along walls, about six spaced at least three feet apart, and checking them each day.

Week four is for sealing, once catches stop. Close the gaps you marked with hardware cloth or rust-proof fill fabric, then watch for new droppings for two weeks. If you still see signs, call us.

Mistakes that keep mice around

Placing traps in open floor space is the most common error. Mice follow edges, so traps belong against the wall with the trigger end toward it.

Setting traps and sealing at the same moment is another. Sealing before the mice are out can trap them in a wall. Using too few traps is a third. A single trap in a room with a population only catches the boldest animal.

Reading the signs by room

In the kitchen, look inside cabinet bases, behind the stove and under the sink, where pipes enter the wall. In the garage, check along the base of the walls and behind stored items. In the attic, look for shredded insulation and droppings along the top plates.

Record what you find in each room. Patterns show where traffic is heaviest and where to place traps first.

Range, breeding and entry size

Texas A&M puts a house mouse's range at roughly 20 to 50 feet and says one mouse can have about 4,500 descendants in a year. A mouse can also use a dime sized hole or a quarter inch crack. Together these show why a nest is usually close by and why early action matters.

Source for "Range, breeding and entry size" (published guidelines): Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, Rodent IPM.

Frequently asked questions.

How many mice does it take to be an infestation?
Even one mouse can breed. Texas A&M says one can have about 4,500 descendants in a year, so we treat any sign seriously.
Why do I only see mice in cold weather?
Cold pushes them toward warmth and food. Many are already nearby, and you notice them when they move in.
Do mice need water?
Texas A&M notes that rats need about an ounce a day.
Is it safe to vacuum droppings?
No. The CDC says not to sweep or vacuum droppings because dust can carry pathogens into the air.
