Rodent control vs. general pest control in Lubbock, why specialists get better results.

Written by Lubbock Rodent Control. Updated November 2026.
By Lubbock Rodent Control · Published November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Rodents are mammals, not insects, and they need different tools: trapping, sealing and cleanup.
  • Sealing gaps matters more than any product. A mouse can use a dime sized hole and a rat a quarter sized one.
  • Rodent work involves droppings and nests, so safe cleanup is part of the job.
  • Ask any provider how they will find entry points and what they will do about them.
  • A rodent specialist can inspect for free and can often be there the same day.

Rodents are not bugs

General pest control is built around insects and spiders, with sprays, baits and monitoring. Rodents behave differently. They are mammals that gnaw, travel along walls, learn to avoid new objects and breed quickly.

That changes the work. Trapping and sealing do far more than spraying.

What good rodent work includes

Texas A&M's integrated approach looks at food, water and shelter together. In practice that means an inspection that finds entry points, trapping or baiting with careful placement, sealing with materials rodents cannot chew, and sanitation advice.

It also includes cleanup. The CDC says to wet clean droppings and not sweep or vacuum, which is a different skill set from treating a kitchen for ants.

Trapping takes patience

Texas A&M advises leaving traps unset with bait for one to two weeks so wary animals get used to them. Traps need to be checked almost daily.

A provider who treats rodents regularly knows this and plans visits around it.

Sealing is the part that lasts

According to Texas A&M, a rat can use a quarter sized hole or a half inch crack, and a mouse about a dime sized one. A plan that does not address those gaps will not last.

Ask what materials will be used. Hardware cloth and rust-proof fill fabric are named in the guide.

Questions to ask before you hire

Ask how they will find entry points, how many visits to expect, what they will use to seal, who handles cleanup and what happens if activity continues. A written plan that answers these is easier to compare.

The Texas Department of Agriculture licenses structural pest control businesses, so it is fair to ask about licensing as well.

When a general pest company is enough

For ants, roaches or spiders, a general pest company is the right choice. For rats and mice, look for someone who works on rodents regularly.

What a first visit should look like

A good rodent inspection takes longer than a quick spray. The technician should walk the outside at ground level, check the roofline, look in the attic or crawl space, and ask where you have seen signs.

You should leave with a clear list: where animals are getting in, what the plan is, what materials will be used and what you need to do. If the visit is only a spray around the foundation, ask why.

Why cleanup should be part of the plan

Droppings, urine and nests are health concerns, and the Texas Department of State Health Services links hantavirus to rodent droppings, urine and saliva. The CDC's wet cleaning steps avoid raising dust.

General pest services often do not include this. If you need it, ask before you sign.

Cost questions without numbers

We do not publish prices on this site because they depend on the building and the problem. What you can ask any provider is what is included, how many visits, what is not included and how follow up works.

We offer a free inspection so you can see the problem and the plan before deciding.

Rats and mice need different plans

A rat needs a larger gap than a mouse, travels farther and behaves differently. Texas A&M puts a Norway rat's range at up to 450 feet and a house mouse's at 20 to 50 feet. A plan that treats both the same is likely to miss something.

Seasonal timing and why speed matters

Rodents breed quickly, so waiting costs more than acting. Texas A&M says one mouse can have about 4,500 descendants in a year. A provider who can inspect the same day, or close to it, can stop that growth earlier.

We offer same-day service when you call early and our line is open 24/7, so you are not waiting for the next business day.

What to prepare before the inspection

Write down where and when you saw signs, and keep any photos. Clear access to the attic hatch, crawl space door and the area under sinks. Put pets in a room away from the work.

If you have already set traps or put out bait, tell the technician where and what you used.

Reading a written plan

A useful written plan names the entry points found, the work to be done at each, the materials, the number of visits and what happens if activity continues. It should also say who does cleanup.

If any of those items are missing, ask. A plan you understand is a plan you can hold someone to.

Comparing providers fairly

When you get more than one plan, line them up item by item. One may include sealing and cleanup while another covers trapping only. A plan that looks smaller may leave out work you will need to pay for later.

Use the same list of questions with each company, and keep notes. Ask for the inspection to be free, as ours is, so you can compare without committing.

Homes versus businesses

A home usually needs a single plan covering the attic, kitchen, garage and exterior. A business may need regular visits, written records and a plan around staff and customers. Ask whether the provider does both and which they do more often.

For commercial work, ask how records are kept and how quickly they can respond if you see activity between visits.

One more question to ask

Ask how the provider will know the job is finished. A good answer is specific: traps stop catching animals, droppings stop appearing, and every gap on the list has been sealed and checked. A vague answer such as 'we will see' is a sign to ask more.
