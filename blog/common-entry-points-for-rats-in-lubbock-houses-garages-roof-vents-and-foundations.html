Common Entry Points for Rats in Lubbock Houses: Roof Vents, Garages, and Foundations

Written by Lubbock Rodent Control. Updated November 2026.
By Lubbock Rodent Control · Updated November 17, 2026 · 8 min read

Understanding where rats enter a Lubbock home is the foundation of an effective exclusion program. Entry points for roof rats and Norway rats are distinct: they access structures at different elevations and through different gap types. This guide covers every common entry point we find during inspections in Lubbock residential construction, organized by location and species.

Roof-line entry points (roof rats).

Roof rats climb, so their entries sit at the roof line and in the attic.

Soffit vents.

We find cracked, missing, or pulled-away vent screening on a majority of Lubbock homes we inspect. Fix: Hardware cloth (1/4-inch galvanized mesh) installed behind or inside the vent frame. Not replacement plastic screening, that will fail again.

Fascia-sheathing gap.

The junction between the fascia board (the vertical board at the roof edge) and the roof sheathing (the plywood or OSB surface the shingles are attached to) develops a gap as the fascia wood dries and shrinks over time. On homes older than 20-30 years, this gap is often 1/2 inch or larger, enough for a roof rat to compress through. It's not visible from the ground; you need to look up from a ladder or from the attic interior to see it. Fix: Sheet metal blocking or wood blocking with mesh, fastened to both the fascia and sheathing surfaces.

Gable vents.

Triangular or rectangular vents at the gable ends of the attic are a less common entry point than soffit vents but are found open in older construction. Same degraded-screening issue as soffit vents, with the same hardware cloth fix.

Pipe and conduit penetrations at the roof.

Water heater flue pipes, dryer vent boots, plumbing vent pipes, and electrical conduit penetrations through the roof surface each have a potential gap around the circumference where the pipe or conduit exits through the roofing material. In older installations, the original boot or flashing may have pulled away from the pipe surface, creating a gap. Fix: Foam backer rod fill plus sealant for smaller gaps; hardware cloth backing before sealing for larger gaps.

Foundation-level entry points (mice and Norway rats).

Mice and Norway rats stay low, so their entries sit at the foundation line and ground level.

Weep holes.

Brick-veneer homes have weep holes, small drainage gaps in the mortar line at the base of the brick, spaced every 24-32 inches around the full perimeter. They're 3/4 inch wide and open directly into the wall cavity. Most homeowners have never heard of them. Most pest programs don't address them. Fix: Copper mesh pressed into every weep hole. Copper specifically, not steel wool, not foam.

Utility pipe penetrations.

Every water supply line, drain pipe, gas line, and electrical conduit that exits through the exterior wall has a potential gap around it. These gaps are typically sealed with caulk at installation, but caulk fails at exterior locations under Lubbock temperature cycling. Check every penetration by feel, if you can fit a pencil into the gap, a mouse can too. Fix: Foam backer rod for fill, exterior-grade sealant over it. For gaps over 1/4 inch, add copper mesh fill before the sealant.

Garage door bottom seal.

The garage door bottom seal is one of the most reliably worn entry points in Lubbock homes. A seal that doesn't contact the floor completely along its full width creates a gap that mice can enter. Worn rubber compresses and cracks over time. Fix: Replace the full-width seal. Close the door and check for light along the entire bottom, if light is visible anywhere, a mouse can get through.

Foundation cracks.

Hairline cracks in the foundation or foundation stucco widen over time and can reach mouse-entry width. Norway rats can exploit foundation-level gaps that are large enough for their head, and their gnawing can widen smaller gaps over time. Fix: Concrete patch for hairline cracks; concrete patch with hardware cloth insert for larger gaps near the grade line.

Norway rat-specific: drain covers and sewer access.

Norway rats in older downtown Lubbock and near sewer-adjacent infrastructure exploit drain cover gaps and worn sewer line joints to access buildings from below. Floor drains with missing or cracked covers, and any drain-cover gap at the foundation perimeter, are potential Norway rat access points. Fix: Replace missing drain covers; seal gaps at foundation-level drain cover perimeters with concrete patch and mesh.

Gap sizes and sealing materials from public guidance

Public health and Extension sources agree on the scale of the problem.

For sealing, Extension names hardware cloth and a rust-proof fill fabric sold in strips for roof lines and similar gaps. The practical rule is to check every roof vent, garage door seal, utility penetration, and foundation gap against those measurements, then seal with material a rodent cannot chew through.

Source: Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, Rodent IPM; CDC, Got Mice? Seal, Trap, and Clean Up (archived). Cited for: gap sizes and sealing materials from public guidance.

The sizes to check

The two numbers worth carrying are from the Texas A&M guide. A mouse can enter through a dime sized hole or a quarter inch crack under a door. A rat needs a quarter sized hole or a half inch crack. Hold a dime or a quarter against any gap and it gives you an answer in a few seconds.

The usual suspects are garage door seals that have worn through, gaps where gas lines and cables enter the wall, dryer and bath fan vents without screens, and the corner where a foundation meets framing. Roof rats add a second set: the roof line, soffit vents and any place a branch or wire touches the house.

Source for "The sizes to check" (published guidelines): Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, Rodent IPM.

A walk-around checklist you can do in an hour

Start outside at ground level and work up. Check the foundation line for burrow openings, since Norway rats burrow near food and water. Check every vent and utility entry for gaps of a half inch or more. Then look at the roof line, where roof rats arrive through vegetation and trees touching the house. Inside, check the garage door seal and the gap under any exterior door against a quarter, which is the size a rat can use. Mark each gap with tape, photograph it and repair it with hardware cloth or rust-proof fill fabric. A second walk after a heavy rain shows what the first one missed, because new burrows tend to appear where water collects.

Source for "A walk-around checklist you can do in an hour" (published guidelines): Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, Rodent IPM.

Why a sealed entry still fails

A repair fails when the material is chewable or when the gap is only partly closed. Check each repair after a week. Rodents gnaw at the edge of a patch, so a crescent of fresh shavings around a plug means the patch is soft or too small. Replace it with hardware cloth sized beyond the gap and fixed on all sides.

Source for "Why a sealed entry still fails" (published guidelines): Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, Rodent IPM.

Safety checklist when you inspect

Inspecting means reaching into gaps and dusty spaces. The CDC advises avoiding dust from droppings, so inspect with care.

What to do:

  • Wear gloves and a mask in attics and crawl spaces.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum droppings.
  • Photograph each gap before sealing.
  • Do not work on a roof alone.

Source for "Safety checklist when you inspect" (published guidelines): CDC, How to Clean Up After Rodents.
