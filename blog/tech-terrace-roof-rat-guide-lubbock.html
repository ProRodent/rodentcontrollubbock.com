Tech Terrace roof rats: why mature trees and older homes draw them.

Written by Lubbock Rodent Control. Updated November 2026.
By Lubbock Rodent Control · Published November 17, 2026 · 6 min read
Open 24/7 Same-day service Locally owned, since 2024 Free inspection

Key takeaways

  • Homes beside large campuses and older buildings face the same pressure points: waste, food and older masonry.
  • Roof rats climb. Branches, wires and fences give them a path to the roof.
  • A rat can use a hole the size of a quarter or a half inch crack, according to Texas A&M.
  • Trim branches away from the roof and seal gaps at the roofline.
  • Wet clean droppings and never sweep or vacuum.

What the Tech Terrace area has in common with other older areas

Texas Tech was founded in 1923, and the Texas Technological College Historic District, about 110 acres, was added to the National Register in 1996. We do not claim where Tech Terrace's blocks begin or what any particular house is built from.

What we can say is practical: homes near a large campus see the same pressures as any older, established part of a city. Waste rooms, food service and older masonry give rodents food, hiding places and gaps to use.

How roof rats get to the roof

Roof rats are good climbers. They use branches that touch the roof, wires, fences and the corners of buildings. Once on the roof, they look for vents, soffits and the places where pipes pass through.

Mature trees are worth keeping, but branches should stay away from the roofline. Cutting back is one of the cheapest steps you can take.

The gap sizes that matter

According to Texas A&M, a rat can use a quarter sized hole or a half inch crack. At the roofline that means gaps at gable vents, soffit returns, flashing edges and around plumbing stacks.

Seal them with hardware cloth or metal flashing. Texas A&M names hardware cloth and rust-proof fill fabric, which is stronger than foam alone.

Signs in the attic

Look for droppings, gnaw marks, nests of shredded insulation and greasy rub marks along beams. Noise overhead at night is a common first clue.

Photograph what you see before you touch anything. Use a flashlight and wear gloves and a mask. Do not work in an attic alone.

Food and water

Roof rats come for food and shelter. Fruit that has fallen, pet food left out and uncovered trash give them reasons to stay. Fix dripping pipes and pooled water too, since Texas A&M notes that rats need about an ounce of water a day.

Move trash cans at least 30 feet from the building when you can, and keep lids tight.

Cleaning up safely

The CDC's method is to ventilate for 30 minutes, wear gloves, wet droppings with a bleach solution of 1.5 cups per gallon, wait five minutes and wipe. Do not sweep or vacuum.

Soaked insulation is better replaced than cleaned. See our attic cleanup service for how that works.

If you hear animals in the attic or find fresh droppings, call. We inspect for free, offer same-day service when you call early and answer 24/7. We are locally owned and have operated since 2024.

A ground level check you can do in ten minutes

Stand at each corner of the house and look up. Do branches touch the roof or the wires leading to it? Does a fence or a shed roof give a step from the ground to the roofline? Those are the routes roof rats use.

Then look at the vents. Gable vents, soffit vents and ridge vents should have intact mesh. If you can see daylight through a gap or a bent screen, note it and photograph it.

What to do about fruit trees and bird feeders

Fruit that falls and is left on the ground is food, and so is spilled seed under a feeder. You do not have to remove the trees or feeders, but pick up what falls each day and use a tray under the feeder.

Compost piles should be kept in closed bins. Open piles are a food source and cover.

When damage is already done

If you find chewed wiring, call an electrician as well as us. Gnawed wires are a fire risk, and sealing the entry does not fix them. If insulation has been nested in or soaked, plan to replace it after the rats are gone.

We can tell you what we see during the inspection and which parts are our work and which need another trade.

Keeping them out after treatment

Trimmed branches, sealed gaps and sealed food storage are what keep roof rats from coming back. Walk the roofline each fall and after any storm that moves branches or damages vents.

If you notice noise again, call early. A small problem is easier to deal with than one that has been there for weeks.

Talking to neighbors

Roof rats do not respect property lines. A neighbor's overgrown tree, a pile of debris or an unsealed shed can feed the problem on your side of the fence. Texas A&M says a Norway rat can range up to 450 feet, and roof rats also travel along fences and wires.

A friendly conversation about trimming and food storage often helps more than any single repair. Share what you found and offer to point out the signs.

A seasonal routine

Pick two dates a year, one in spring and one in fall, to walk the property. In spring, check what winter weather moved or damaged: loose vents, fallen branches, torn screens. In fall, repeat the full check before cold weather arrives.

Keep your photos from each walk in one folder, labeled by date. If something changes, you will see it. Write down the date you last cut back branches and the date you last checked the attic.

Frequently asked questions.

Do roof rats damage wiring?
They gnaw, and wiring is one of the things they can chew. Texas A&M lists gnaw marks among the signs of rodent activity.
Should I trim trees?
Yes. Branches that touch the roof are a path. Keep them away from the roofline.
Can I seal the gaps myself?
Small gaps can be sealed with hardware cloth. Do not seal until you are sure the rats are out.
